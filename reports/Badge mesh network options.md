# Quieter radios let hundreds of hearts sync

The Herz-Schwarm swarm is limited by airtime on one 2.4 GHz channel, not by ESP-NOW, the chip or the lack of mesh software. Each 17-byte broadcast holds the channel for about 0.72 ms at ESP-NOW's default 1 Mbps. At 20 packets a second per badge the channel fills at about 70 badges and stays clean only up to about 20, and 300 badges would offer roughly 430 % of a channel. None of the off-the-shelf meshes fixes this: ESP-WIFI-MESH, ESP-Mesh-Lite, painlessMesh, BLE Mesh, Thread, Zigbee, Meshtastic and MeshCore all add traffic and latency. The fix is a protocol change on the existing hardware. Send a timebase at 2–5 Hz and render the 20 Hz beat locally, as WLED does. Use 6 Mbps OFDM frames, skip redundant beacons, and spread effects as hop-limited, deduplicated state gossip, as the r0ket badge did at Camp 2011. The same schedule unlocks battery life. On the new battery badge the always-on receiver draws 93 of the 160 mA in the brightness-capped typical case, and ESP-NOW wake windows measured on an ESP32-S3 cut board current from 103 to 45–50 mA. By my estimate that lifts runtime from 6.0 h to roughly 8–10 h. Range should come from a raised base station and a few relays, using ESP-NOW Long Range mode or an 868 MHz LoRa downlink. Short badge-to-badge range in a crowd is an asset, not a defect. Changing chips buys little: an ESP32-C6 saves about 10 mA and an ESP32-C5 gains about 1 dB of link budget. Only the ESP32-H2 and Nordic parts cut radio current 4–25×, and they cannot speak ESP-NOW. The project should keep the ESP32-S3FH4R2 board for Camp 2027 and spend the coming months on three things: an instrumented load test with the 12 existing badges, a protocol v2, and a beat-locked listen-window prototype. No recent event badge mesh, from Supercon 2025 to BornHack 2026, has published delivery data, so the project's own logs would be the first.

## Airtime, not ESP-NOW, caps today's protocol near 20 badges

ESP-NOW sets no limit on swarm size. The 20-entry peer table, with at most 17 encrypted peers, applies only to unicast. Asked about more than 1,000 ESP32-S3 boards, Espressif staff said a broadcast to FF:FF:FF:FF:FF:FF reaches every ESP in range "independent of how many that are" ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=134934)). Broadcast frames cannot be encrypted ([ESP-IDF ESP-NOW API](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-reference/network/esp_now.html)). The real ceiling is airtime. Each ESP-NOW frame adds about 43 bytes of MAC header, vendor element and checksum to the payload, and the default rate is 1 Mbps ([ESP-IDF ESP-NOW API](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-reference/network/esp_now.html)). A 192 µs DSSS long preamble comes first ([Keysight](https://helpfiles.keysight.com/csg/89600B/Webhelp/Subsystems/wlan-dsss/content/trc_time.htm)). By that arithmetic the badge's 17-byte `SyncPacket` holds the channel for about **0.72 ms**, including the DIFS gap. Twelve badges at 20 Hz use about 17 % of channel 13, which is why the current swarm works. Three hundred badges would offer about **430 % of a channel**. The swarm fails long before full load: broadcasts have no ACK or retry, and badges spread across a field cannot all hear each other, so the channel behaves like unslotted ALOHA. In that model a frame survives with probability e^(−2G): about 55 % at 30 % load and 82 % at 10 %. Losses compound across the crowd: even at 99 % per badge, a one-shot broadcast reaches all 300 badges only **4.9 %** of the time (0.99^300), so commands must be state that is re-broadcast until superseded (my calculations).

There is little evidence about large ESP-NOW swarms. Espressif's own reliability test used only 8 nodes sending 4 packets a second inside a building, and judged about half of them reliable ([Espressif reliability blog](https://developer.espressif.com/blog/reliability-esp-now/)). In a forum report, 13 ESP32-C3 boards broadcasting 130 messages a second in total lost about 20 % of packets, at only about 9 % airtime, and Espressif staff blamed congestion ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=146575)). That loss at such low airtime suggests the receive path can saturate before the air does. A fresh search in October 2026 still found no published measurement with more than 13 nodes. The table shows how many badges one channel carries at a 30 % load target, by my arithmetic from the frame format; random backoff adds airtime, so these are best cases.

| PHY rate | Airtime per 17-byte packet | 20 Hz | 5 Hz | 2 Hz | 1 Hz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Mbps DSSS (today) | ~0.72 ms | ~21 | ~83 | ~210 | ~415 |
| 6 Mbps OFDM | ~0.15 ms | ~95 | ~375 | ~940 | — |
| Long Range 250 kbps | ≥1.9 ms | ~7 | ~28 | ~70 | ~140 |

Three levers move the swarm toward the right-hand columns. The largest is **packet rate**. Since firmware 1.2.2 the heartbeat is already a shared phase counter ([todo.md](../todo.md)), so it does not need 20 samples a second. WLED broadcasts a timebase (`millis() + timebase`) and lets each receiver render the effect in phase without per-frame traffic ([WLED udp.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wled/WLED/main/wled00/udp.cpp)). Espressif's `espnow_time` module re-broadcasts authoritative time only every 30 s ([espnow_time docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-now/en/latest/esp32/api-reference/time/espnow_time.html)). Payload is nearly free: a frame's fixed cost (about 536 µs) dwarfs the 8 µs each payload byte adds, so packing more state into fewer packets costs almost nothing, while every extra packet costs a lot.

The second lever is **PHY rate**. `esp_now_set_peer_rate_config()` can switch broadcasts to 6 Mbps OFDM ([ESP-FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-faq/en/latest/application-solution/esp-now.html)). That cuts airtime about fivefold and costs 5.2 dB of sensitivity, −93.2 against −98.4 dBm on the S3 ([ESP32-S3 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s3_datasheet_en.pdf)), a good trade when most neighbours are tens of metres away.

The third lever is **suppression and scheduling**. Under Trickle, a node stays quiet if it has already heard k copies of the same message, so traffic grows only logarithmically with density ([RFC 6206](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6206)); k = 1–5 is typical ([arXiv 1509.08664](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.08664)). DESYNC's self-organising TDMA cut message loss on Telos motes from about 58 % to under 1 % ([Harvard TR-18-06](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/25680331/tr-18-06.pdf?sequence=1)). Hash-based slotting alone fails: hashing 300 badges into 800 slots leaves about 31 % sharing a slot, so slots need listen-before-claim re-selection.

The proximity effect sets a floor under the packet rate. RSSI from chest-worn badges is noisy. In a bus study, swapping only the people holding the phones changed attenuation by about ±10 dB ([arXiv 2006.08543](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08543)), and RSSI "need not decrease with increasing distance" ([arXiv 2006.06822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.06822)). A usable near/far signal needs a median over several packets per neighbour. That argues for 2–5 Hz beacons at 6 Mbps rather than 1 Hz at 1 Mbps, and at 5 Hz and 6 Mbps about 375 badges fit the 30 % budget. The firmware has its own limit: the neighbour table holds 25 entries, silently ignores newcomers when full, and drops a neighbour after 4 s of silence ([Herz_Schwarm_CCC_V.ino](../Herz_Schwarm_CCC_V.ino), `MAX_PEERS`, `PEER_TIMEOUT`). Both values need rethinking for a crowd that hears hundreds of badges at a few hertz. Conveniently for testing, today's 12 badges already put 240 frames a second on the air, the load of 240 badges beaconing at 1 Hz.

## No off-the-shelf mesh survives 300 badges at 20 Hz

"Mesh" points the project in the wrong direction. Routed meshes exist to carry unicast data across several hops. The swarm mostly needs a one-hop broadcast plus an occasional flood, and every routed option adds traffic and latency.

ESP-WIFI-MESH builds a tree with one root attached to a router. It adds 10–30 ms per hop, takes up to 60 s to form and up to 10 s to heal after the root fails, and Espressif tested it with 100 devices ([ESP-WIFI-MESH guide](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-guides/esp-wifi-mesh.html)). ESP-Mesh-Lite is faster per hop (8–12 ms), but repairs take up to 45–50 s and its published test used 50 devices ([Mesh-Lite User Guide](https://github.com/espressif/esp-mesh-lite/blob/release/v1.0/components/mesh_lite/User_Guide.md)). painlessMesh runs TCP and JSON over a combined SoftAP and station link, defaults to four connections per node, and asks users to "be conservative" with broadcasts ([painlessMesh README](https://github.com/gmag11/painlessMesh)). All three keep a SoftAP radio on all the time, which rules out the ESP-NOW sleep modes covered below, and none copes with nodes that keep walking around.

Switching radios does not escape the problem. BLE Mesh is managed flooding over the same three advertising channels. Espressif quotes under 1 kbit/s of throughput and about 100 tested nodes ([Espressif mesh comparison](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-techpedia/en/latest/esp-friends/solution-introduction/mesh/mesh-comparison.html)), and simulations show loss rising again once a mesh reaches about 106 devices ([arXiv 1910.03345](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.03345)). Thread caps at 32 routers ([OpenThread](https://openthread.io/guides/thread-primer/node-roles-and-types)), and Espressif measured 4–5 KB/s of UDP throughput ([Espressif mesh comparison](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-techpedia/en/latest/esp-friends/solution-introduction/mesh/mesh-comparison.html)). 300 badges at 20 Hz would generate about 102 KB/s of payload (my arithmetic). Zigbee's broadcast transaction table effectively limits a whole network to about one broadcast per second ([Digi](https://docs.digi.com/resources/documentation/digidocs/90001539/concepts/c_broadcast_transmissions.htm)).

Plain BLE advertising behaves much like ESP-NOW broadcast. Apply Ruuvi's pairwise collision model ([Ruuvi](https://ruuvi.com/?p=2908)) to a 0.3 ms legacy advert sent at 20 Hz by 300 badges in range: a scanner receives a given badge's advert only about 3 % of the time, rising to about 84 % at 1 Hz (my arithmetic). Coded PHY uses seven times the airtime, which makes crowds worse. PAwR, the scheduled-response mode in Bluetooth 5.4 that scales to thousands of receivers, is unsupported on the ESP32-S3 and experimental on the C6 ([ESP-IDF BLE status, S3](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-guides/ble/ble-feature-support-status.html); [C6](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32c6/api-guides/ble/ble-feature-support-status.html)). Fri3d Camp 2026 is the closest recent analogue, with about 700 ESP32 badges on BLE beacons. With default scan settings a badge heard 2 beacons in 12 s; with an explicit 50 % scan window it heard 44 ([steeman.be](https://steeman.be/posts/building-fri3d-friends/)).

LoRa meshes fail on regulation and airtime together. Most EU 868 MHz sub-bands allow each device a 0.1–1 % duty cycle, and only 869.4–869.65 MHz allows 10 % ([ETSI EN 300 220-2](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300200_300299/30022002/03.02.01_60/en_30022002v030201p.pdf)). By the Semtech formula, a 20-byte packet at SF7/125 kHz takes about 57 ms on air ([SX1261/2 datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/6/b/5/1/4/SX1262_datasheet.pdf)), so one badge at 20 Hz would need 113 % of the channel. Meshtastic's DEF CON 33 mesh of more than 2,000 nodes ([Meshtastic blog](https://meshtastic.org/blog/that-one-time-at-defcon/)) needed event firmware with the SHORT_TURBO preset and a 3-hop cap ([event firmware announcement](https://damus.io/nevent1qqspan9u2je53kztsvwen2n7m76t8f9gxpnnl9yxu8n35lwuhter5wq9dpw4n)). Turbo presets are not offered in the EU_868 region ([Meshtastic LoRa config](https://meshtastic.org/docs/configuration/radio/lora/)). MeshCore, the MIT-licensed C++ alternative, saves airtime by switching to learned paths after a first flood, but its channel (broadcast) messages, exactly the swarm's traffic pattern, always flood ([MeshCore](https://github.com/meshcore-dev/MeshCore); [EastMesh wiki](https://wiki.eastmesh.au/meshcore/routing/)); transport codes can now scope a flood to a region, which bounds its spread but not its airtime ([MeshCore packet format](https://github.com/meshcore-dev/MeshCore/blob/main/docs/packet_format.md)). By my arithmetic, a 300-node EU mesh carries one broadcast per node every few minutes.

Three recent event badges are the closest precedents, and none published delivery, latency or battery data. Hackaday Supercon 2025's roughly 500 ESP32-S3 + SX1262 badges ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2025/10/23/announcing-the-2025-hackaday-superconference-communicator-badge/)) ran a **bounded flood**, not a blind repeater: TTL 3, a duplicate cache keyed on a 16-bit checksum, a skipped repeat once another badge was heard relaying, CAD before sending, and relayed packets shed while the queue was half full ([net.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/badge/net/net.py); [firmware README](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/README.md)). The radio used 500 kHz, SF7 and 9 dBm with the PA set low, and carried only typed chat ([lora.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/bc5d473b/firmware/badge/net/lora.py); [chat.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/bc5d473b/firmware/badge/apps/chat.py)). The organisers say it "worked flawlessly, even on microwatts" but give no numbers ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2026/02/18/2026-hackaday-europe-call-for-participation-we-want-you/)). That validates the design proposed here only under light load: one 128-byte chat message a second, relayed three times, would already fill about 22 % of the channel (my arithmetic). **WHY2025**'s roughly 2,500 badges carried an SX1262 that was unusable as shipped: Meshtastic needed two bodge wires, and any LoRa firmware needed a hand-soldered antenna connector and displaced the ESP32-C6's Wi-Fi firmware ([MeshCore PR #671](https://github.com/meshcore-dev/MeshCore/pull/671); [WHY2025 wiki](https://wiki.why2025.org/Project:Meshtastic_on_the_WHY2025_badge)). No source says how many joined a mesh. **BornHack 2026**'s Cyber Ægg, an nRF52840 + SX1262 badge, shipped as a MeshCore node out of the box, on the stock EU/UK Narrow channel with client repeat off ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2026/07/17/the-bornhack-2026-cyber-aegg-is-a-badge-with-a-life-afterwards/); [firmware README](https://codeberg.org/Ranzbak/bornhack-firmware-2026)).

What works is older and simpler. The r0ket at Camp 2011 gossiped state, not events. Each badge stored ten 32-byte records and re-broadcast the whole store on a timer jittered between 0.5× and 1.5× the nominal interval, kept only the newest-timestamped record for each message type, reset the whole swarm through a generation byte, and obeyed a network-wide "meshnice" value that made badges skip sends at random ([r0ket mesh.c](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/r0ket/r0ket/master/firmware/funk/mesh.c); [mesh.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/r0ket/r0ket/master/firmware/funk/mesh.h)). Airtime per badge stays fixed however many effects are running, and "newest wins" deduplicates for free. Espressif's `esp-now` component offers matching flooding tools: a hop limit (`forward_ttl`), an RSSI floor below which packets are not forwarded (`forward_rssi`), and a per-packet `magic` ID for deduplication. It also repeats every frame 10 times by default, and its own header warns that too many retransmissions congest the network ([espnow.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/espressif/esp-now/master/src/espnow/include/espnow.h)). Commercial crowd LEDs avoid the problem: receivers never transmit and are steered by IR or RF from a central transmitter ([PixMob FAQ](https://pixmob.com/faq); [Xyloband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xyloband)).

Badges also need a leaderless fallback for when no base is in range. CrowdClock badges simply adopt any higher clock they hear ([Hackster](https://www.hackster.io/tony-goacher/self-synchronising-led-badges-using-esp-now-dfa2b0)), and the CHIRP thesis showed firefly-style sync on ESP32-S3 with ESP-NOW converging "within the first few minutes" ([UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/gradstudies_etd_2026/200)).

Two operational lessons carry over from Meshtastic. At Hamvention 2024 one MQTT bridge flooding traffic collapsed the mesh ([Wikipedia: Meshtastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meshtastic)), so the base and relays need hard rate limits. At DEF CON the shared-key channel allowed anyone to spoof node information ([Meshtastic blog](https://meshtastic.org/blog/that-one-time-at-defcon/)).

ESP-NOW broadcasts cannot be encrypted, so base packets that trigger a virus or a swarm reset need authentication. A shared HMAC key is weak, since dumping one badge's flash yields it. With an **Ed25519 signature** badges hold only a public key, and the packet, with an 8-byte counter, still takes only about 0.23 ms at 6 Mbps (my arithmetic). An HMAC needs **at least 8 bytes**; MeshCore's 2-byte MAC, forgeable at random once in 65,536 tries, is the counter-example ([MeshCore packet format](https://github.com/meshcore-dev/MeshCore/blob/main/docs/packet_format.md)). Against replay, the base should persist a boot epoch and number every packet, as ESPHome's rolling code does ([ESPHome packet transport](https://esphome.io/components/packet_transport/)), and badges, which reboot without state, should also reject packets more than a few beats from swarm time. A badge that has seen authenticated base packets must never fall back silently to unauthenticated ones, the flaw behind Meshtastic's CVE-2025-53627 ([CVE record](https://cveawg.mitre.org/api/cve/CVE-2025-53627)). Supercon shipped the remote throttle this enables, a signed CONFIG_OVERRIDE that changes any setting on every badge, but its RSA-1024 signature covers only key and value, so a recorded override can be replayed ([config_manager.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/badge/apps/config_manager.py); [crypto.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/badge/net/crypto.py)).

## Range belongs on masts, not in louder badges

On paper the S3 has a strong link budget: 21 dBm transmit power and −98.4 dBm sensitivity at 1 Mbps ([ESP32-S3 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s3_datasheet_en.pdf)). The EU caps 2.4 GHz transmitters at 20 dBm EIRP ([EN 300 328 glossary](https://ib-lenhardt.com/kb/glossary/en-300-328)). In open fields Espressif measured ESP-NOW with PCB antennas as near-lossless to 150 m and about 60 % delivery at 300 m. Long Range mode was near-lossless to 450 m ([Espressif outdoor blog](https://developer.espressif.com/blog/esp-now-for-outdoor-applications/)). A walk test with trees and fences found 30 % loss common beyond 50–100 m ([atomic14](https://atomic14.com/videos/posts/oz0a7Ur7nko)). Bodies cut range sharply. A person 0.3 m from the receiver adds a median 17 dB when facing the link and 11 dB side-on, and up to 34 dB in the worst case ([IEEE 802.15-08-0434](https://mentor.ieee.org/802.15/dcn/08/15-08-0434-00-0006-path-loss-due-to-human-body.pdf)). One person in the path adds about 10 dB and two about 15 dB ([Januszkiewicz 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6211019/)). Badge-to-badge links in a crowd therefore close over tens of metres. The base already hears badges at only −85 to −95 dBm, a margin of 3–13 dB ([todo.md](../todo.md)).

Short range is a feature: each badge only needs its neighbours, and a small collision domain lets distant parts of the field reuse the same airtime and keeps proximity effects local. Badges stay at 13 dBm because of heat ([todo.md](../todo.md)). Raising them to 21 dBm would gain 8 dB, about 2.5× the free-space range, but would also enlarge every badge's collision footprint.

Range belongs instead in a few fixed nodes: the base and two to six relays mounted above head height with external antennas, re-broadcasting base packets with a TTL of 1–2. CCC has done this before. Sputnik at 24C3 tracked about 1,000 coin-cell tags through more than 30 fixed base stations ([CCC Sputnik](https://events.ccc.de/congress/2007/Sputnik/index.html)). Relays should count their hops apart from the badge TTL, as Meshtastic's zero-cost hops let favourite routers relay without spending hop budget ([Meshtastic blog](https://meshtastic.org/blog/zero-cost-hops-favorite-routers/)), and each should send the base a heartbeat. One power level needs correcting: the base and bridge transmit 21 dBm into the antenna ([todo.md](../todo.md)), over the 20 dBm EIRP limit with any antenna gain of −1 dBi or more, so on a mast the antenna gain must come off the transmit power.

ESP-NOW Long Range mode suits this downlink role. It is an Espressif-proprietary PHY at 250 or 500 kbps that gains about 4 dB of sensitivity and works on the S3, C3, C6 and C5 but not the C2 ([ESP-IDF v5.3 Wi-Fi guide](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/v5.3/esp32s3/api-guides/wifi.html)). The current driver overview documents a combined 802.11 BGNLR mode in which an LR-enabled station stays compatible with ordinary Wi-Fi, and LR follows standard CCA and backoff ([ESP-IDF Wi-Fi driver overview](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-guides/wifi-driver/overview.html)). Espressif's claim of 2–2.5× the distance is optimistic: 4 dB is a factor of 1.58 in free space, so 1.5–2× is realistic for body-worn badges. LR frames last at least three times as long as 1 Mbps frames, so the mode suits a few long links, not beacons from hundreds of badges.

The open question is whether one badge can receive LR and normal-rate ESP-NOW frames at the same time, which the docs still do not address. One ESP32-C3 user got an "invalid rate" error when enabling a mixed set of protocols ([esp32.com](https://esp32.com/viewtopic.php?p=116681)), but that resembles an acknowledged bug where setting an LR rate before `esp_now_init()` asserted ([esp-idf#11751](https://github.com/espressif/esp-idf/issues/11751)), so it is weak evidence. The project's own note that LR would have to be switched on all devices at once reflects the same concern ([todo.md](../todo.md)). This needs a bench test before any design work.

The 868 MHz band gives the most range per milliwatt for the same downlink. The 869.4–869.65 MHz sub-band allows 500 mW ERP at a 10 % duty cycle ([ETSI EN 300 220-2](https://www.etsi.org/deliver/etsi_en/300200_300299/30022002/03.02.01_60/en_30022002v030201p.pdf)). By my arithmetic, a base at that power using LoRa SF7/125 has about 44 dB more link budget than ESP-NOW at 20 dBm. An SX1262 receives at only 4.6–5.3 mA ([SX1261/2 datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/6/b/5/1/4/SX1262_datasheet.pdf)).

There are two ways to use it. Putting a receiver in every badge costs a $3.90–6.60 module ([Heltec HT-RA62](https://heltec.org/product/ht-ra62/); [LCSC E22-900M22S](https://www.lcsc.com/product-image/C411293.html)) plus an antenna, roughly 55–90 % on top of the $8.91 BOM ([BOM.md](../herz_badge/BOM.md)). The antenna is the weak point. A quarter wave at 868 MHz is 8.6 cm long, far larger than the 30 × 34 mm board, and even a dedicated 868 MHz chip antenna averages −4 dBi on its vendor's evaluation board ([Johanson](https://www.johansontechnology.com/docs/4384/Antenna-0868AT43A0020001E-2.0.pdf)), which has a bigger ground plane than the badge. The cheaper way leaves the badges unchanged and links the base to ESP-NOW relays over LoRa, with boards such as the Heltec WiFi LoRa 32 V4 at $17.90–27.50 ([Heltec V4](https://heltec.org/project/wifi-lora-32-v4/)). Such a board needs no driver work if it runs MeshCore's MIT-licensed **KISS modem** firmware on a UART to the base or a relay's ESP32: it carries raw frames of up to 255 bytes and can set the radio, test for a busy channel, estimate airtime and read the noise floor ([kiss_modem_protocol.md](https://github.com/meshcore-dev/MeshCore/blob/main/docs/kiss_modem_protocol.md)). Its 500 ms default TX delay must come down, and the host must enforce the duty cycle.

Either way the backbone will have neighbours. Meshtastic's EU_868 region uses the same sub-band ([Meshtastic LoRa config](https://meshtastic.org/docs/configuration/radio/lora/)), Hackaday Europe 2026 ran the Supercon badges at 869.525 MHz/250 kHz ([commit 5b0ee746](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/commit/5b0ee746)), and MeshCore's official EU/UK Narrow preset, which BornHack's badges use, sits at 869.618 MHz/62.5 kHz/SF8, with the older 869.525 MHz SF11 preset marked deprecated ([MeshCore presets](https://api.meshcore.nz/api/v1/config)). Attendee nodes will use these channels, so the sub-band needs an **on-site survey** before the backbone is configured, with an SX1262 RSSI sweep like Supercon's Spectrum app ([spectrum.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/badge/apps/spectrum.py)) or the KISS modem's noise-floor command.

Spectrum at the camp is the last variable for range. Channel 13 is legal in the EU ([RTR](https://www.rtr.at/TKP/was_wir_tun/telekommunikation/spectrum/bands/2400MHz/Spektrum2400MHz.en.html)). But a 1 Mbps DSSS signal on channel 13 still overlaps an AP on channel 11, and Espressif notes that ESP-NOW frames are received from adjacent channels ([espnow.h](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/espressif/esp-now/master/src/espnow/include/espnow.h)). The 37C3 network ran 2.4 GHz on channels 1, 5, 9 and 13 ([37C3 network info](https://events.ccc.de/congress/2023/infos/network.html)), a plan that would put camp APs on the badges' channel, although 96 % of clients were on 5 GHz at peak at 36C3 ([36C3 Infrastructure Review](https://amara.org/subtitles/6qFbavdMs60r/en/2/download/36C3%20-%20%2036C3%20Infrastructure%20Review.en.srt)). The firmware should set its country code explicitly, and the team should agree on a channel with the camp NOC.

5 GHz ESP-NOW on an ESP32-C5 is no fix for 2027. Espressif's docs conflict on whether it works at all ([ESP-NOW C5](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32c5/api-reference/network/esp_now.html); [Wi-Fi overview C5](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/stable/esp32c5/api-guides/wifi-driver/overview.html)), it loses about 10 dB of link budget against 2.4 GHz at 1 Mbps (path loss plus a 6 Mbps minimum rate, my arithmetic), and every badge would have to be a C5.

## The always-on receiver is 58 % of the new badge's battery

The new board changes where the power goes. Its WS2812B-2020-V6 LEDs draw 1 µA each when dark ([DESIGN.md](../herz_badge/DESIGN.md)). Classic WS2812Bs draw 0.4–1 mA per dark LED ([PJRC](https://www.pjrc.com/how-much-current-do-ws2812-neopixel-leds-really-use); [LowPowerLab](https://lowpowerlab.com/forum/general-topics/ws2812b-rgb-led-measurements/)), 26–64 mA for 64 LEDs, but only on the Waveshare boards. The repo's simulation gives 233 mA and 4.1 h on the 1000 mAh cell in a typical crowd, and 160 mA and 6.0 h with brightness capped at 60. The ESP32-S3 accounts for 93 mA in both ([runtime.md](../herz_badge/sim/results/runtime.md)). With the brightness cap, the always-on receiver is **58 % of the battery budget**, and the LEDs draw only 67 mA. Transmitting barely matters: at 13 dBm, 20 broadcasts a second cost about 1 mA ([DESIGN.md](../herz_badge/DESIGN.md)).

Within Espressif's Wi-Fi line, no chip changes the picture. The best, the C6, saves about 10 mA, 11 % of the radio's draw. Moving the S3 from ESP-NOW to BLE makes things slightly worse, because BLE receive costs 93 mA against 88 mA for Wi-Fi.

The real lever is on the existing silicon. Since ESP-IDF 5.0, `esp_now_set_wake_window()` together with `esp_wifi_connectionless_module_set_wake_interval()` switches the receiver on only for a window at the start of each interval. Transmitting still works at any time, as long as the chip runs in station mode ([ESP-IDF power save](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-idf/en/latest/esp32s3/api-guides/wifi-driver/wifi-performance-and-power-save.html); [ESP-FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-faq/en/latest/application-solution/esp-now.html)). Measured on an ESP32-S3 FeatherS3, board current fell from 103 mA with the radio always on to 50 mA at 10 % duty and 45 mA at 2 % duty. With the radio fully off it was 43.8 mA ([glenn20](https://github.com/glenn20/upy-esp32-experiments/tree/main/ESPNowPowerManagement)). The remaining floor is the CPU. The datasheet puts modem-sleep at 22 mA at 80 MHz and 32.9 mA at 240 MHz, with peripheral clocks off ([ESP32-S3 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s3_datasheet_en.pdf)). By my arithmetic, cutting the S3's 93 mA to 25–50 mA on the same 1000 mAh cell would lift the capped typical-crowd runtime from 6.0 h to roughly **8–10 h**, and the quiet case from 7.5 h to roughly 11–16 h.

The catch is synchronisation. Espressif states that sender and receiver windows must be synchronised at the application layer, and that data sent outside a window is lost ([ESP-FAQ](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/espressif-esp-faq/en/latest/application-solution/esp-now.html)). No documented API sets the window's phase when the badge is not connected to an access point, so locking it to the beat needs a bench test. Espressif's own ESP-NOW Matter bridge sidesteps phase: it listens 110 ms of every 200 ms, and senders repeat at gaps shorter than the window until the repeats span the off-time ([esp-matter bridge docs](https://github.com/espressif/esp-matter/blob/main/examples/bridge_apps/esp-now_bridge_light/docs/esp-now-bridge-with-button.md)). By my arithmetic from the FeatherS3 figures, such windows would put the S3 near 76 mA, about **7.2 h** of capped runtime, but each neighbour would hear only about 55 % of beacons, so RSSI medians need 1.8× as many. The rule is therefore a fallback, well suited to rare base commands at 2–3 repeats each, while beat-locked windows remain the target. Windows also clash with capacity: 300 badges each sending one 0.72 ms frame into a shared 50 ms window need 216 ms of airtime. At 6 Mbps they take about 45 ms, and only a rotating subset should beacon in each window. The measured currents also come from MicroPython on ESP-IDF 5.0.2, not the Arduino-ESP32 3.x core the badge uses.

A LoRa receive-only badge would end up in the same place. By my estimate from the datasheets, an S3 in modem-sleep plus an SX1262 draws roughly 25–48 mA ([SX1261/2 datasheet](https://cdn.sparkfun.com/assets/6/b/5/1/4/SX1262_datasheet.pdf); [ESP32-S3 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s3_datasheet_en.pdf)), and it would still need 2.4 GHz listening windows for proximity. Wake windows give comparable savings with no BOM change.

Only a different radio class breaks through this floor. An ESP32-H2 receives at 24–25 mA ([H2 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-h2_datasheet_en.pdf)) and a Nordic nRF54L15 at about 3.4 mA ([Nordic](https://www.nordicsemi.com/Products/nRF54L15)). On a Nordic badge the LEDs would be nearly the whole load. But neither chip speaks ESP-NOW, so the 12 existing badges, the base and the bridge would all need a new protocol. The Nordic parts also top out at +8 dBm ([nRF52840 PS](https://infocenter.nordicsemi.com/pdf/nRF52840_PS_v1.8.pdf)).

## Keep the ESP32-S3 for Camp 2027 and rewrite the protocol

The ESP32-S3FH4R2 board should stay. It is designed and costed, with the chip at $2.31 in quantities of 1,000 ([LCSC](https://lcsc.com/product-detail/image/ESP32-S3FH4R2_C3013940.html)). Every change that matters is firmware or infrastructure: packet rate, PHY rate, suppression, listen windows and fixed relays.

If the board is respun anyway, the ESP32-C6 is the only low-risk swap. The C6FH4 costs $1.64–2.06 ([Future Electronics](https://www.futureelectronics.com/p/esp32-c6fh4-C917616295); [Findchips](https://findchips.com/search/ESP32-C6-Zero)). Arduino-ESP32 lists it as stable ([Arduino-ESP32 docs](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/arduino-esp32/en/latest/getting_started.html)). The project's bridge already runs on C3 or C6 next to S3 badges ([todo.md](../todo.md)). FastLED supports it, but its RMT peripheral has no DMA, and FastLED itself recommends the older RMT4 driver if Wi-Fi causes flicker ([FastLED README](https://github.com/FastLED/FastLED)). The other candidates do not fit: the C61 lacks an RMT peripheral ([C61 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-c61_datasheet_en.pdf)), the H4 is available only as samples ([Espressif](https://www.espressif.com/en/node/10417)), the S31 datasheet is a pre-release ([S31 datasheet](https://documentation.espressif.com/esp32-s31_datasheet_en.pdf)), and the XIAO nRF54L15 has no Arduino IDE support and runs Zephyr under PlatformIO ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/xiao_nrf54l15_with_platform_io)).

PlatformIO's official `espressif32` platform still ships Arduino-ESP32 2.x, so version 3.x and the C5/C6 need the community pioarduino fork ([PlatformIO forum](https://community.platformio.org/t/using-espressif-arduino-esp32-v3-2-0-with-platformio/47629); [CNX Software](https://cnx-software.com/2024/08/27/platform-espressif32-fork-platformio-arduino-esp32-c6-esp32-c5-esp32-h2-esp32-p4/)). The project's `arduino-cli` builds avoid that problem.

ESPHome added an `espnow` component in 2025.8 ([ESPHome 2025.8.0](https://esphome.io/changelog/2025.8.0/)), but its `on_broadcast` trigger fires only for registered peers, it has no ESP-NOW encryption, it follows the Wi-Fi channel whenever Wi-Fi is on, and it exposes neither Long Range mode nor wake windows ([ESPHome ESP-NOW](https://esphome.io/components/espnow/)). That suits a base or bridge, not badge firmware. ESPHome's `sx126x` component (2025.7) sends and receives raw LoRa packets from YAML lambdas, an alternative to the KISS modem that makes a LoRa base or relay a configuration job ([ESPHome 2025.7.0](https://esphome.io/changelog/2025.7.0/); [ESPHome SX126x](https://esphome.io/components/sx126x/)). It does not enforce the duty cycle either.

No off-the-shelf board combines a badge-sized LED matrix with a battery. The M5Stack Atom Matrix has 25 LEDs and no battery ([M5Stack docs](https://docs.m5stack.com/en/core/ATOM%20Matrix)), and the Ulanzi TC001 is a desk clock ([Elektor](https://www.elektor.com/products/ulanzi-tc001-esp32-based-smart-pixel-clock)), so the custom board is justified. For prototypes, a XIAO ESP32-C6 at about €5–6, with a LiPo charger and a switchable ceramic or u.FL antenna, suits a cheap test fleet ([Seeed wiki](https://wiki.seeedstudio.com/xiao_esp32c6_getting_started/); [Pi Hut](https://thepihut.com/collections/seeed?page=1)). The XIAO ESP32S3 + Wio-SX1262 kit at about $10 suits LoRa receive experiments ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/?p=139035)). A Heltec V4, a Wireless Stick Lite V3 at €19.50 ([Tinytronics](https://www.tinytronics.nl/nl/development-boards/microcontroller-boards/met-lora/heltec-wireless-stick-lite-v3-lora-868mhz-esp32-s3-sx1262)) or a RAK3312 at $15 ([RAK store](https://store.rakwireless.com/products/wisblock-core-module-rak3312-lora-wifi-ble)) suits relays.

Two camp failures set hardware rules. WHY2025's badge carried two unprotected 18650 cells able to push more than 20 A into a short, their traces 0.4 mm from ground under 10–30 µm of solder mask; in a 20 A test the protection reacted too slowly and a MOSFET glowed hot ([WHY2025 fire hazard advisory](https://wiki.why2025.org/Badge/Fire_hazard)). Supercon's SMA connectors snapped off because only one side had been soldered ([issue #53](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/issues/53)). The battery badge lists no fuse, and its BOM only says to ask the vendor for a protection board ([BOM.md](../herz_badge/BOM.md)), so everything rests on the cell's own protection.

The work should run in this order:

| Step | Work | Done when |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Load test (now) | Run the 12 badges at 20, 50 and 100 Hz with sequence numbers. Compare 1 Mbps with 6 Mbps. Log loss and RSSI spread per neighbour through 3–5 ESP32 sniffers feeding a database and dashboards. | A loss-versus-offered-load curve exists, and RSSI medians at 2–5 Hz are stable enough for near/far. |
| 2. Protocol v2 | Timebase beacon at 2–5 Hz and 6 Mbps, jitter of 0.5–1.5×, Trickle suppression (k = 2–3). Effects as newest-wins state records with TTL ≤ 3, an RSSI floor and a zone byte. Dedup on sender + 24–32-bit sequence; relays back off ≥ 1 frame time and count infrastructure hops separately. Generation byte, signed "nice" knob, Ed25519 or ≥ 8-byte HMAC plus epoch and counter on base packets, rate limits on base and bridge. Larger `MAX_PEERS` and a longer `PEER_TIMEOUT`. | 12 badges sync and spread effects at under 10 % airtime. |
| 3. Listen windows | Beat-locked wake window, CPU at 80 MHz, light sleep between LED frames, on the new board; the phase-free 110/200 ms rule as fallback. | Measured ESP32 share at or below 50 mA. |
| 4. Range infrastructure | Base and bridge on a mast with an external antenna at ≤ 20 dBm EIRP, plus 2–6 relays with heartbeats. Bench-test LR reception alongside 1 Mbps. Survey 869.4–869.65 MHz on site before picking a LoRa channel. | A measured 10 dB or better margin at the edge of the site. |
| 5. Spectrum | Agree a 2.4 GHz channel with the camp NOC and set the country code in firmware. | Channel confirmed in writing. |
| 6. Hardware | Keep the S3 board and protect the antenna keep-out. Require protected cells, checked on arrival; insulate the cell and VBAT pads. Strain-relieve antenna connectors on base and relays. Add LoRa to badges only after an 868 MHz antenna test on a 30 × 34 mm board. | Go/no-go recorded in DESIGN.md. |

The load test comes first because it costs nothing and targets the largest unknown, how ESP-NOW behaves beyond 50 nodes. At 100 Hz the 12 badges put about 1,200 frames a second on the air, 86 % of the channel. That matches the airtime of 60 badges at 20 Hz or 1,200 badges at 1 Hz. It does not reproduce hidden terminals or the receive-side cost of tracking hundreds of distinct senders, so the camp will see at least the loss measured here, not less. The test should use the camp's instruments: ESP32-S3 sniffers in promiscuous mode logging time, sender, length, rate and RSSI for every frame into TimescaleDB and Grafana, as stridetastic does for Meshtastic ([stridetastic](https://github.com/0wulf/stridetastic)), which yields airtime per second, badges heard per sniffer and loss per sequence number. Paxcounter cannot help: libpax counts only randomised MAC addresses, so it never counts an ESP32 badge ([libpax.cpp](https://github.com/dbinfrago/libpax/blob/master/lib/libpax/libpax.cpp)).

Two of step 2's rules answer flaws in Supercon's code, by my reading: its send routine fires 0–10 ms after the channel clears without re-checking, so every badge queued behind one packet transmits within about two LoRa preambles, and a 16-bit checksum cache held for 100 minutes yields false duplicates once thousands of messages have passed ([net.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/main/firmware/badge/net/net.py); [lora.py](https://github.com/Hack-a-Day/2025-Communicator_Badge/blob/bc5d473b/firmware/badge/net/lora.py)).

## Conclusion

The three goals turn out to be one design decision. A predictable, low-rate, scheduled beacon is the capacity fix, because going from 20 Hz at 1 Mbps to 2–5 Hz at 6 Mbps cuts airtime roughly 20–50×. It is the battery fix, because a receiver can only sleep when it knows when to listen. And it is what makes range infrastructure possible, because relays inject into known slots. The firmware is already halfway there: 1.2.2 introduced a shared phase counter and 1.4.0 a common wave start time. What remains is to stop transmitting what every badge can already compute. The question "which mesh?" goes away once it is clear the swarm needs a clock and bounded gossip, not routing.

The deeper implication concerns sequencing. A different radio class (ESP32-H2, nRF54L15) only pays off after the protocol is schedule-based, because a scheduled ESP-NOW badge already gets most of the radio savings, and then the LEDs dominate the budget again. Silicon is a question for the camp after 2027, not for 2027. Meanwhile the decisive unknowns can all be tested on the 12 badges the project already owns, within weeks: loss beyond 50 nodes, control over wake-window phase, LR coexistence, and the camp's 2.4 GHz channel plan.
